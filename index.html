Campus Mobility & Occupancy Analytics System

The full engineering report for my BS Software Engineering final year project at GCUF (Session 2022–2026). The system turns ordinary CCTV or webcam footage into live people counts, occupancy tracking and crowd-surge alerts. Supervisor: Sir Numan-ul-haq.

Technical documentationSRS and UMLDatabase designYOLOv8DeepSORTFastAPIReactSupabase
What the report covers

The problem

Counting people at campus entrances by hand is slow and differs from one observer to the next. Most campuses already have CCTV, but it is only used for watching or for reviewing incidents. Our project gives that footage a numeric signal that can support planning, staffing and safety.

What we built

A full-stack web platform that runs on a mid-range laptop with no dedicated GPU. YOLOv8 detects people, DeepSORT tracks them, and a virtual-line counter records an IN or OUT event only when someone actually crosses the line, which prevents the same person being counted twice. Annotated video reaches the browser as a continuous stream, and rolling one-minute recordings let an operator scrub back through the past. The dashboard shows live occupancy, incoming and outgoing counts, hourly trends, gate comparison, peak hours and a rush alert based on a statistical Z-score.

Chapters

1. Introduction: scope, objectives, process model
2. Literature review: background, tools, related research
3. Analysis and design: requirements, use cases, activity, sequence, class, component and deployment diagrams
4. Database design: data dictionary, DFDs, ER diagram, normalisation
5. Interface snapshots: administrator and user screens
Also included: tests at unit, integration, end-to-end and acceptance level, and a clear scope boundary that excludes face recognition and behaviour detection for privacy reasons.

From Webcam to Crowd Counter: How Our Campus System Works

A plain-language article that explains a complex project to non-technical readers.

Technical blogExplaining complex ideas
Read the article

Every campus has cameras. Very few use them to answer a simple question: how many people are here right now? Counting by hand at a gate is slow, and two people standing side by side will often get two different totals. For our final year project, we set out to let the cameras count instead.

Step 1: Find the people

The system uses a detection model called YOLOv8 to spot people in each video frame. Because many entrances are dim, we first brighten the image with a technique called CLAHE, and we ignore boxes that are far too big or too small to be a person.

Step 2: Follow them

A tracker called DeepSORT gives each person an identity number and follows them from frame to frame. Without it, the computer would see a fresh crowd in every picture.

Step 3: Count only real crossings

Early counting methods simply counted whoever was visible, so a person who was briefly lost and found again was counted twice. We borrowed an idea from commercial people-counting cameras instead. A virtual line is drawn across the entrance, and a person adds one to IN or OUT only when they actually cross it. Each person can add at most one IN and one OUT.

Step 4: Show it clearly

A web dashboard displays current occupancy, arrivals, departures, hourly trends and a comparison between gates. A statistical check watches the last ten minutes and raises a rush alert when traffic jumps well above normal. Recorded one-minute clips let a staff member slide back in time and watch what happened.

What it deliberately does not do

The system counts people. It does not recognise faces or try to detect fights, because those tasks need different technology and raise privacy concerns that go beyond crowd counting. That boundary was a design choice, and we think it makes the project easier to trust.

Quick-Start Guide: Using the Occupancy Dashboard

Remote Work and Productivity: What Really Changes When the Office Disappears

Part of a five-blog series on the benefits and challenges of remote work in modern businesses.

Blog writingProductivityOriginal content
Read the full blog

For decades, productivity had a place: a desk, a building, a manager walking past. When millions of people moved their work home, businesses learned that where we work matters less than how we work. Remote work is neither a perk nor a problem on its own. It is a different way of organising effort, and it rewards the companies that plan for it.

Why productivity can rise

The strongest evidence comes from a well-known experiment by Stanford economist Nicholas Bloom and colleagues at the travel company Ctrip. Call-centre employees who worked from home for nine months performed about 13 percent better than office colleagues, partly because they took fewer breaks and sick days and worked in a quieter setting.

Three things explain most of the gains. First, there is no commute, which gives people back time and energy. Second, a home workspace has fewer casual interruptions than an open-plan office. Third, remote work pushes teams to judge results instead of hours. A designer who delivers a finished layout by Thursday has done the job, whether she worked from 9 to 5 or from 7 to 2.

Where productivity slips

Remote work also has real costs. Home can be noisy, shared, or short on a good desk and a reliable internet connection. Some people find it hard to stop working, so the day stretches. Others lose focus without the structure an office gives them. New hires suffer most, because they pick up habits by watching colleagues, and a video call is a poor substitute for sitting nearby.

Isolation is the challenge people mention most often. Without casual chats, employees can feel cut off from the team and from the purpose of their work. Over time, loneliness lowers motivation, and motivation is the heart of productivity.

Communication is the hinge

In an office, a quick question takes ten seconds. Remotely, the same question can sit unanswered for hours, and tone is easily misread in a short message. Teams that work well at a distance treat communication as a system rather than a habit. They agree which tool is used for what: chat for quick questions, email for formal decisions, a shared task board for progress. They write messages that make sense on their own, with the context, the request and the deadline included.

Time zones add a further layer. A team spread across several countries cannot rely on live meetings alone, so written updates and recorded walkthroughs become essential. GitLab, a company that has operated fully remotely since its early days, is often cited because it publishes its handbook openly and relies on documentation so that nobody has to wait for an answer from a colleague who is asleep.

Practical strategies that work

Set clear goals. Give each person a small number of measurable outcomes each week. When goals are visible, trust grows and micromanagement fades.

Create routines. A short daily check-in, a weekly planning call and a fixed end-of-day time keep the team in rhythm and protect personal time.

Make space for people. Open a chat channel for non-work talk, hold occasional virtual coffee breaks, and meet in person once or twice a year if the budget allows. These small touches work directly against isolation.

Invest in the basics. Reliable video software, shared documents and a simple project board cost far less than the confusion they prevent. Offering a stipend for internet or a desk chair also sends a strong message that the company cares about how work is done.

Train managers. Leading a remote team is a skill. Managers need to learn how to give feedback in writing, notice signs of burnout, and run meetings that respect everyone’s time.

The effect on the organisation

When these habits are in place, the gains reach the whole business. Companies can hire from a wider talent pool, reduce office costs, and often see lower staff turnover because employees value flexibility. When they are missing, the damage is just as visible: duplicated work, missed deadlines and quiet resignations. Organisational performance therefore depends less on the remote model itself than on the quality of management around it.

Conclusion

Remote work can raise productivity, but only when companies match its freedom with clarity, communication and care. The office is no longer the place where work happens by default. It is one option among several, and the smartest businesses will choose the mix that fits their people and their goals.

Source consulted: Bloom, N., Liang, J., Roberts, J. and Ying, Z. J. (2015). Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 130(1), 165–218.
